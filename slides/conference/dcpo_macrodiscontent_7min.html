Rising or Trendless?

Athens, Syntagma Square, 2011 (Kotsolis, CC BY-SA 3.0)  |  Santiago, Plaza Baquedano, 2019 (Carlos Figueroa, CC BY-SA 4.0); via Wikimedia Commons

A rising tide?

  • Democratic deconsolidation (Foa and Mounk 2016, 2017)
  • Erosion of political support (Dalton 2004; Jennings et al. 2017)

Or just noise?

  • “Trendless fluctuations” (Norris 2011)
  • Discontent fluctuates without a clear trend

Research Question

Is public discontent with the political system rising? And what drives it?

What I’ll offer

  1. A bird’s-eye view of political discontent
  2. A method for cross-national, over-time comparison
  3. A systematic analysis of what drives discontent

Athens 2011, protests against austerity; Santiago 2019, the estallido social. Both are OECD democracies, both were sparked by economic grievances, and both appear in our data. [click] Scholars disagree on a basic fact: Foa and Mounk see democratic deconsolidation; Norris sees only trendless fluctuations. [click] So our question: is discontent with the political system rising, and what drives it? [click] In seven minutes I offer three things: a bird’s-eye view of discontent, a method for comparing it across countries and time, and a systematic test of its drivers.

Why the Debate Persists

Conceptual fragmentation

  • Lack of diffuse support (Easton 1975)
  • Low responsiveness (Jennings et al. 2016)
  • Democratic deficit (Norris 2011)
  • Incumbent dissatisfaction (Muller and Jukam 1983)

Data constraints

  • Incomparable: different questions, projects, rounds
  • Sparse: many country-years never asked
  • Different datasets → conflicting conclusions (Jennings et al. 2017)

Our solution

  • Diffuse support: system legitimacy → threat when eroded

Included

  • External efficacy/responsiveness
  • Trust in political institutions
  • Perceived political corruption

Excluded

  • Trust in incumbent (specific~)
  • Support for democracy
  • Satisfaction with democracy

Discontent matters because legitimacy underpins democratic survival, and discontent feeds populism. But the debate persists for two reasons. First, scholars define discontent differently: lack of diffuse support, low responsiveness, democratic deficits, dissatisfaction with the government. Second, survey data are incomparable and sparse, so different datasets give different conclusions. [click] We follow Easton: discontent is a lack of diffuse support for the system as a whole, not dissatisfaction with the current government. It includes low external efficacy, low trust in political institutions, and perceived corruption. It excludes trust in incumbents and satisfaction with democracy, which behave like specific support, and abstract support for democracy, which is near universal.

Solving the Data Constraints

388 survey datasets  |  111 items  |  136 countries  |  56 years

  • Solution: Dynamic Comparative Public Opinion (Solt 2020b)
  • Output: PPD scores, 3,362 country-years → publicly available
  • Validated: source items, democracy evaluations, government performance

Incomparability

  • Difficulty: discontent needed to endorse a response
  • Dispersion: noise of each item
  • Country-specific bias: translation, culture

Sparsity

  • Random-walk prior: PPDt = PPDt-1 + shock
  • Borrows strength across years
  • Cost: wider uncertainty in thin years

We collected 388 surveys with 111 relevant items, covering 136 countries from 1968 to 2023. But no single question is asked everywhere, every year. [click] Our solution is the DCPO latent-variable model. Think of educational testing: students take different test forms, yet their abilities land on one common scale. DCPO treats each survey item as its own imperfect instrument, estimating how hard it is to endorse, how noisy it is, and how it is biased in each country. A random-walk prior fills in thinly surveyed years. The result: public political discontent, or PPD, scores for 3,362 country-years, publicly available. And they pass three validity checks: they track the original survey items, they correlate with democracy evaluations we never used, and with views of government performance. Details are in the appendix.

What Drives Discontent: Economy over Institutions

Driver Expected Found

Election year ↓ ↓ modest
Power-sharing ↓ n.s.

38 OECD countries, 1,217 country-years
Bayesian multilevel, within-between (Bell and Jones 2015)

We test four explanations together on 38 OECD democracies, 1,217 country-years, with a Bayesian multilevel model that separates long-run differences between countries from short-run changes within them. First, the trend: net of everything else, discontent is rising, by about 0.1 points a year. Not trendless fluctuation. Elections were expected to give discontent an outlet; they do lower it, but only modestly. Power-sharing institutions, parliamentarism and federalism, were expected to make citizens feel included; they make no detectable difference. The economy is the strongest driver: richer countries are much less discontented, and rising unemployment or falling growth pushes discontent up immediately. Inequality was ambiguous in theory; rising inequality actually lowers discontent, consistent with system justification and relative power theories. The full long-run versus short-run breakdown is in the appendix.

Conclusion

  1. Concept: discontent as lack of diffuse support
  2. Measure: PPD for 136 countries over 56 years, validated three ways
  3. Trend: OECD discontent is rising, not trendless fluctuation
  4. Drivers: economic conditions matter more than elections and institutions

Contributions

Theory: one framework for fragmented measures of discontent
Data: open panel for backsliding, populism, and regime stability

Anything that can increase social and institutional trust produces especially large benefits for those subject to various forms of hardship.

—— Helliwell et al., World Happiness Report 2020, Ch. 2

To sum up: a clearer concept, a global validated measure, a rising trend, and an economic story. [click] The data are open for research on backsliding, populism, and regime stability, in democracies and non-democracies alike. [click] Why should this matter for wellbeing? The World Happiness Report finds that institutional trust cushions people against hardship, offsetting on average about 40 percent of the wellbeing loss from risks such as unemployment and low income. Our finding is the mirror image: economic hardship erodes that very trust. So hardship hurts twice, and the people who need the cushion most are the first to lose it. Tracking political discontent is part of tracking human wellbeing.

Hypotheses

Political

  • H1: Elections
    • Outlet → PPD ↓
    • Negative campaigns → PPD ↑
  • H2: Power-sharing institutions → inclusion → PPD ↓

(Lau et al. 2007; Kittilson and Schwindt-Bayer 2010; Lijphart 1999)

Economic

  • H3: Low income, slow growth, unemployment → PPD ↑
  • H4: Income inequality
    • Grievance → PPD ↑
    • System justification, relative power → PPD ↓

(Quaranta and Martini 2016; Jost 2019; Solt 2008)

Validation: External Convergent

  • Items not used in estimation: evaluations of democracy → R = −0.76 to −0.84

ISSP National Identity: proud of how democracy works (R = -0.77). WVS/EVS: satisfied with how democracy is developing (R = -0.84). CSES: satisfied with the way democracy works (R = -0.76). These items tap specific rather than diffuse support, so they were excluded from estimation.

Validation: Construct

  • Government success in health, elderly care, environment → R = −0.58 to −0.68

ISSP Role of Government 2006 and 2016: health care (R = -0.58), the elderly (R = -0.64), the environment (R = -0.68).

Research Design

Data

  • 38 OECD countries, 1,217 country-years
  • Elections: DES (Bormann and Golder 2022)
  • Institutions: parliamentarism, federalism, Gallagher index
  • Economy: OECD.Stat; Gini: SWIID (Solt 2020a)

Model

  • Bayesian multilevel: country and year intercepts (Shor et al. 2007)
  • Within-between: long run vs. short run (Bell and Jones 2015)
  • Measurement uncertainty in PPD and Gini (Tai et al. 2024)
  • Best single item (Eurobarometer) → 582 obs., Europe only

1,217 country-years; 16 (Turkey) to 43 (US) consecutive years per country, mean 32. Within-between random effects: each time-varying predictor is split into its country mean (long-run effect) and the yearly deviation from that mean (short-run effect). PPD and Gini enter as distributions with known standard deviations. Estimated with brms.

Long Run vs. Short Run

Predictor Long run: between countries Short run: within country
GDP per capita ↓ ↓
GDP growth n.s. ↓
Unemployment ↑ ↑
Income inequality n.s. ↓
Disproportionality ↑ n.s.
  • Bad economy → discontent ↑ at both horizons
  • Rising inequality → discontent ↓: system justification, relative power

GDP per capita: a country 1 SD above the mean has PPD 9.4 points lower than one 1 SD below (95% CI: 14.8 to 4.1). Short run: 0.7 points less per 2 SD increase (85.6% of posterior negative). GDP growth: no long-run effect; short run, 1.2 points less per 2 SD increase (95% CI: 1.8 to 0.6). Unemployment: long run 3.9 points (95% CI: -0.8 to 8.5); short run 3.8 points per 2 SD increase (95% CI: 3.2 to 4.4). Income inequality: short run, 1.1 points lower per 2 SD rise (95% CI: 1.8 to 0.5). Disproportionality: two SD higher mean Gallagher index means 3.6 points more PPD (94.9% of posterior positive). Time trend: 0.1 points per year (95% CI: 0.04 to 0.16). Election years: 0.5 points lower (95.6% of posterior below zero).
