Research Question

Citizens resent public servants’ privileges,
yet compete ever harder to become one. Why?

  • Top: Phoenix News, September 2024. A county in Shandong reports 8,000 public servants receiving 800 million yuan in pensions.
  • Bottom: The Economist on a pay rise for government workers that sparked “anger and envy.”
  • The welfare gap between insiders and outsiders is now highly visible on social media, down to the county level.
  • Yet nobody is marching against the public service system. People are queuing to get in.

Two Reactions, One Society

Resentment

  • County report: 8,000 retirees, 800 million yuan pensions
  • Bilibili videos: 800,000+ views (Vesipjia 2025)
  • Screens flooded with angry comments

Competition

  • NPSE 2026: 3 million+ applicants
  • 92 applicants per vacancy
  • Gaokao: only 2.7 per seat (Dai 2025)

What evaluative framework lets both reactions coexist?

  • April 2025: the story “8,000 public servants receiving 800 million yuan in pension funds” spread swiftly online.
  • The National Public Service Examination set a historical record in 2026.
  • The concrete question: when ordinary citizens discuss public servants, what framework do they rely on?
  • And what does that framework imply for the public service system and for the resilience of the regime?

Co-optation and Its Interest-Based Account

  • Co-optation → resilience via buyoffs (Svolik 2012; Gandhi and Przeworski 2006, 2007)
  • China’s defining feature: meritocracy (Ang 2016; Jin 2023; Bai and Jia 2016)
  • Leading account (Liu 2024, 2019)
    • Open examination → belief in upward mobility
    • Mobility belief → redistributive grievance ↓

What about the excluded majority?

For most citizens, entry remains a “remote prospect”.

  • The interest-based logic explains why aspirants comply. It says little about the many who will never get in.
  • Our argument: co-optation rests not only on the compliance of the privileged few but, more fundamentally, on the tacit consent of the excluded many.

Why Interest Alone Falls Short

  1. Co-opted share shrinks as the bureaucracy expands (Guo 2006)
  2. Examination is far from fully open (Ho 1967; Jiang and Kung 2021; Elman 2000)
  3. Positions grow scarcer: 2026 NPSE quota cut by 1,602 (Zhang 2025)

→ Durable institutions need socio-psychological foundations (DiMaggio and Powell 1983; March and Olsen 1983)

  • Historical evidence on keju: the proportion successfully absorbed declined over time.
  • Rising class stratification lets the exam favor those who already hold educational and social capital.
  • 2026 was the first reduction in recruitment since 2019.
  • The sociological-institutionalism critique of rational choice: stable socio-psychological foundations, not transient interest, sustain institutions over the long run.
  • Without knowing whether such identification exists, we cannot say when co-optation works or whether it survives once material incentives weaken.

Social Cognition of Public Servants (SCPS)

  • Social cognition = normative expectations of a target (Bandura 1987; Frith and Frith 2012)
    • Shaped by peers, track record, culture (Sherman et al. 1989; Hjortskov 2020; Olsen 2017)
  • “Public servant” in daily use = symbol of state power
  • Two levels: individual official ↔︎ institution

The moral twist

Faulting an official’s character invokes the system’s own norm.
Criticism of the individual → reaffirms the institution

  • The legal definition is narrow (staff in the state administrative staffing), but everyday usage applies “public servant” to all state-funded personnel, with ordinary officials as the mental image.
  • We follow social usage.
  • Normally individual and institution blur: citizens read the system through officials’ conduct.
  • When complaints turn moral, the two pull apart. The bad official becomes a deviation from an institutional ideal.
  • This cognition is the social-psychological ground on which co-optation rests.

What Existing Research Misses

Mainstream: behavior(Jiang 2018; Li and Zhou 2005; Landry et al. 2017)

  • Weberian lens on bureaucrats
  • Performance, promotion, incentives

Partial lenses on SCPS

  • Anti-public-sector bias (Chen et al. 2024; Gao et al. 2024)
  • Street-level bureaucrats (Lipsky 2010; Hanser 2016)
  • Blame in crises (Wang and Zhang 2024; Zhang and Hu 2023)

Gap

The overall framework citizens apply to public servants remains unknown.

  • Most studies ask how formal and informal mechanisms drive job performance or shape promotion.
  • The anti-public-sector bias work reads attitudes through bureaucratic rigidity; street-level work examines frontline implementation logic.
  • Limited data access and technical thresholds kept earlier work from capturing platform-visible evaluation (Neo et al. 2023).
  • Dense citizen-bureaucrat interaction in China gives ordinary people their own vocabulary for judging officials (Shue 1988; Walder 1988). That is the interpretive autonomy we foreground.

Three Expected Dimensions

Job performance

  • Source: occupational role
  • What insiders deliver
  • State capacity (Fukuyama 2013; Zacka 2022)

Welfare distribution

  • Source: individual interest
  • What insiders receive
  • Rational choice (Downs 1957; Jin 2023)

Moral character

  • Source: political culture
  • Who insiders ought to be
  • Virtue over ability (Schwartz 1985)
  • Performance: reform-era public service aimed to fix the inefficiency of the old cadre system (Burns and Wang 2010). Performance legitimizes executive power that does not come from elections.
  • Welfare: citizens compare insider benefits with their own. Stable salaries and redistributive advantages drive exam-taking, especially among high achievers (Bai et al. 2025; Chen 2024).
  • Moral: in the Chinese tradition, virtue (德) is a defining attribute of officials (Yang and Rutgers 2017). Virtue outweighed competence in selecting talent because a virtuous official edifies others. This departs from the Weberian ideal (Van der Wal and Yang 2015).
  • Marxist theory: socialist cadres form a vanguard defined by political awareness and moral motivation; CPC cadre selection puts virtue first (Schurmann 1968).
  • All three plausibly coexist. Which predominates is an empirical question.

Hypotheses

Public-servant discourse features…

  • H1: job performance
    • “Lying flat” (tangping), service quality
  • H2: welfare distribution
    • Salary, benefits, job stability
  • H3: moral character
    • Integrity, uprightness, incorruptibility
  • The hypotheses are about presence. The coding scheme weights the three dimensions equally, so the data, not the coding rule, decide which one predominates.
  • Rationalist and performance-based priors would put welfare or performance on top.

Weibo Public Servant Dataset 2024

  • 100 days from the 2024 NPSE
  • 8 keywords: 公务员, 体制内, 考公…
  • News and spam removed
  • 165,835 posts and comments
  • Window: 26 November 2023 to early March 2024. Keywords: 公务员, 体制内, 铁饭碗, 考公, 选调, 央选, 国考, 省考.
  • Exam-driven discussion is broad and less exposed to event-, audience-, or location-specific confounds.
  • It is also a demanding test: attention concentrates on exams, so only patterns strong enough to recur across diverse discussions will surface.
  • Peaks on 26 Nov 2023 (exam day) and 13 Jan 2024 (score release) confirm the corpus tracks genuine attention.
  • Scope: Weibo participation is selective and visibility is shaped by moderation and self-censorship. We characterize publicly expressed, platform-visible discourse, not private beliefs of the population.

Two-Step Design

Full corpus: STM

  • Structural topic model, K = 21 (Roberts et al. 2014)
  • 7 covariates of topic prevalence
    • Content tag, complexity (CTTR), account type…
  • Human coding of topics
    • Fleiss’ κ = 0.841

Case contexts: keyness + LLM

  • 4 policy reform debates
  • Top 30 χ2 words → posts (Gabrielatos 2018)
  • LLM coding of posts (Heseltine and Clemm von Hohenberg 2024)
    • DeepSeek, cross-checked by ChatGPT-o3
    • Few-shot + chain-of-thought
  • STM lets document metadata inform topic prevalence and performs well on short texts (Kwon et al. 2019).
  • Covariates: text length, verification type, account level (Gold V, Orange V, individual), post date (polynomial), content tag (22 categories), linguistic complexity, sensitivity.
  • Three coders saw the 15 FREX words and 30 example documents per topic and assigned it to performance, welfare, moral, or none.
  • STM rests on a bag-of-words assumption (Blei et al. 2003); the case step restores context.
  • LLMs code consistently and match human experts on short sociopolitical texts. Prompting used few-shot examples and CoT (Wei et al. 2022).

Four Policy Debates

Case 1: Exam eligibility for criminals’ children

  • Rule-of-law reform vs. lianzuo legacy

Case 2: Delayed retirement

  • Aging and pension pressure

Case 3: Exit mechanism for teachers

  • Shiyebian, parallel to public servants

Case 4: Lifting the age-35 ceiling

  • Cadre-rejuvenation rule since 1994

→ Long-standing rules, cutting across different interests

  • Case 1: current rules disqualify candidates whose immediate family committed serious crimes. Reformers (e.g., CPPCC member Zhou Shihong, Prof. Luo Xiang) call it incompatible with rule of law.
  • Case 2: still under deliberation in our window; passed September 2024, effective January 2025.
  • Case 3: falling birth rates leave localities with surplus teachers.
  • Case 4: the age-35 rule spread from cadre selection to private firms. In October 2025 the public service ceiling was raised to 38.
  • Each case links to public-service recruitment or welfare, and each would hit different groups differently.

Who Speaks, and How

  • Posts are short: median five tokens, maximum 53.
  • Linguistic complexity is stratified; simple language dominates.
  • Ordinary individual users write more than 80% of posts; high-level verified accounts contribute little.
  • Weibo flags about two-thirds of posts as sensitive.

Three Dimensions, Unequal Weight

  • Examination: 7 topics
    • Expected in the NPSE window
  • All three dimensions present
  • Share of corpus
    • Performance: 11.75%
    • Welfare: 15.82%
    • Moral: 35.53%
  • We added an Examination dimension to separate exam talk (difficulty, score anxiety, hope of passing). Its prominence confirms the model tracks what it should.
  • H1 to H3 are all supported in the sense of presence.
  • Performance is the smallest: work motivation and calls to end “lying flat.”
  • Welfare runs hot but bounded: it holds the highest-proportion non-exam topic (salary, pension, SOEs) yet spans only three topics. Welfare grievance is intense, but it stays about welfare.
  • Against rationalist and performance priors, moral character commands the highest share and the widest spread.

What Moral Posts Say

Conduct read as a sign of character

  • Privileged behavior: Topics 6, 17, 10
  • Lapses in private life and at work: Topics 20, 21
  • Beyond public office: Topics 7, 8
    • E-sports players mocked as “public servants”
    • Writers judged by “within-system” standards

The moral standard travels beyond its nominal targets.

  • Most moral posts mark departures from a standard of integrity and incorruptibility.
  • Topic 7: users brand certain e-sports players “public servants” to mock preferential treatment despite mediocre play.
  • Topic 8: debate over a blogger’s claims about the political leanings of a famous writer’s work, judged by the rule that “writers and public intellectuals within the system should show stronger political consciousness than ordinary citizens.”
  • None of these people holds public office.

The Moral Yardstick Spreads Widely

Moral topics × content tags

  • Entertainment, culture, judiciary
  • Fashion, healthcare, public security
  • Attaches to any subject

Performance and welfare topics

  • Tied to own subjects
  • Childbirth, housing, finance, sports
  • Dots are average estimated coefficients of content tags on topic prevalence; only significant coefficients are shown. Model numbers match STM topics.
  • Greater tag diversity signals wider reach across social-media contexts.

Moral Talk Is Blunt

Linguistic complexity

  • Moral ↓
  • Performance ↑
  • Welfare ↑

Account type

  • Influencers ≠ users
  • When disappointment and moral judgment take over, people write in blunt, emotional terms rather than structured argument.
  • Issues tied to personal interest invite reasoned cases, so complexity rises with performance (Topic 12) and welfare.
  • Moral judgment surfaces as immediate reaction more than worked-out argument.
  • Against Orange V accounts, personal and Gold V accounts carry opposite signs on every topic except Topics 3 and 13. Influencers can diverge from ordinary users, not only track official discourse (Zhang et al. 2018).

A Cohesive, Autonomous Cluster

Discussion enters the moral dimension rarely leaves

  • Topics behave like concepts; even a short post can hold several at once (Hu 2020). We computed marginal correlations between topic prevalences (Roberts et al. 2019).
  • Edge thickness = correlation strength (multiplied by five for visibility); only positive correlations shown.
  • Right panel: colored edges link topics within a dimension, gray edges link across dimensions.
  • Once an issue is cast in moral terms, it tends to stay there.

Cases: Moral Character Leads in Three of Four

  • Each point is the number of posts the LLM assigned to a category; uncertain posts go to “Other.”
  • Case 1: 1,587 moral posts vs. 422 on systemic policy impact.
  • Case 2: welfare leads (566) but moral discussion is substantial (166).
  • Cases 3 and 4: 201 and 125 moral posts.
  • The shared expectation: public servants as “exemplary role models” (模范表率) of moral purity.

Case 1: Guarding Purity

Once children become high-ranking officials, overturning cases for family members becomes effortless.

— Weibo post, Case 1

  • Keep “morally deficient” groups out
  • Judge candidates by their parents
  • Equal opportunity rarely invoked
  • Many posts reason that flawed parents raise corrupt children who will abuse office once admitted.
  • Offered a more open and fair path into public service, the public does not reach first for equal opportunity. It calls for punishing or excluding the morally suspect.

Cases 3 & 4: Lead by Example

Case 3: teacher exit

Public servants, as the vanguard serving the people, should lead by example and take the initiative, setting a precedent first.

  • Start exit inside the public service

Case 4: age-35 ceiling

Public service recruitment should be liberalized first, otherwise how can private enterprises reform?

  • Public service must lift it first

Same “exemplary role” standard, now demanding reform

  • Case 3: posts insist exit should start with public servants, whether or not the shiyebian system is actually less efficient.
  • Case 4: people press on the roots of unequal institutions and argue reform wins acceptance only if the public service exam lifts the age limit first. Another typical post: “Public service recruitment should take the lead and assume a leadership role in this matter!”
  • In Case 1 the standard argued against reform; here it argues for it. The moral standard is a resource people think with.

Case 2: Welfare Grievance, Moral Voice

Those within the system naturally wish they could work until their seventies or eighties.

Welfare strand

  • Private workers dismissed before retirement age
  • Policy irrelevant to outsiders

Moral strand

  • Reform as a self-serving plot

Pension resentment → impugning character

  • Many posts sit between the two strands: “Those within the system all prefer late retirement. Those outside the system find it hard to say,” or “Extending the age is for the benefit of public servants.”
  • The LLMs coded these as welfare, which is why welfare outnumbers the rest here.
  • Even where welfare leads the count, the grievance speaks in moral terms.

A Moralized Evaluative Grammar

Distributive grievance

↓

Moral judgment of individual officials

↓

“The system ought to produce virtuous servants”

↓

Resentment voiced, institution unchallenged

  • Delayed retirement shows the mechanism in miniature: pension-gap resentment surfaced not as a demand to dismantle the system but as suspicion about the officials who stood to gain.
  • To condemn a corrupt or self-serving public servant is to reaffirm the expectation that the system should produce virtuous ones.
  • This explains why discontent over insider-outsider inequality coexists with fierce competition to enter.
  • Why moral rather than material? In a system citizens cannot easily contest head-on, grievance gravitates toward the register that is culturally licensed and institutionally safe.

Rethinking Co-optation

Interest-based view

  • Incentives for insiders
  • Mobility belief (Liu 2024)
  • Silent on the excluded

Cognitive view

  • Tacit consent of the excluded many
  • Complaint targets individuals
  • Institution reproduced through complaint

Legitimacy rests on how publics reason,
not only on what institutions distribute.

  • Co-optation rests not only on incentives to insiders but on a shared cognitive structure that reproduces the institution through the very idiom of complaint.
  • For political legitimacy broadly: durable institutions can be sustained by how publics are disposed to reason about them, not only by distribution or coercion.

Culture and State-Society Relations

Culture as active grammar

  • Not a static residue
  • “Exemplary role model” argues both sides
  • Comparable virtue grammars elsewhere?

Citizens as category authors

  • Beyond state responsiveness (Hu et al. 2025)
  • Citizens moralize public roles
  • People-centered policy may still fail
  • Culturalist explanations are criticized for treating values as fixed residues. Here, people on opposite sides of the same reform reach for the same standard: one side to demand stricter exclusion, the other faster liberalization.
  • A standard that argues for and against a policy is doing interpretive work.
  • Comparative question: Confucian-influenced bureaucracies and other traditions that moralize public office.
  • Responsiveness research asks how states react; it overlooks how citizens build the categories through which they judge state action.
  • Case 1 illustrates: reformers framed it as equal opportunity and legal fairness; citizens saw a threat to moral purity.

Pressure from Below

Public servants squeezed

  • From above: administrative burden (Xiao and Zhu 2022; Li and Yu 2026)
  • From below: moral expectations
  • Self-interest read as moral failing

Why moralize?

  • Sense of injustice?
  • Feeling of powerlessness?
  • Envy of insider security?

Stabilizer today → tension generator tomorrow?

  • Positioned between state and society, public servants absorb demands from both.
  • The psychological source of moralization is hard to disentangle in platform-visible discourse; our corpus cannot adjudicate.
  • What we establish is the societal outcome: a moralized grammar reaching both sides of the insider-outsider divide. It presses on public servants and disciplines the excluded majority.
  • If that pressure finds no release, the mechanism that contains insider-outsider tension could one day generate it.

Conclusion

  1. SCPS has three dimensions; moral character predominates
  2. Moral talk is pervasive, blunt, and self-contained
  3. “Exemplary role model” frames both sides of reform
  4. Grievance → individuals, institution spared

Theoretical contribution: a moralized grammar sustains co-optation
Practical implication: public servants face pressure from below

The stability it produces, though real, leaves little room for complacency.

  • Answer to the puzzle: a moralized evaluative grammar lets resentment find voice without becoming institutional opposition.
  • Disciplinary perspective: ordinary citizens keep interpretive autonomy under authoritarian rule, authoring the categories through which they judge the state.
